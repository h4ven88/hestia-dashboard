# Hestia v2.0.0 Release Notes

**Release Date:** 2026-09-26

---

## Why this is 2.0.0

Nothing about using Hestia changes in this release. What changes is how your settings are stored when they sync through hestari.com, and it changes at the foundation rather than at the edges. That is worth a major version on its own, and it is worth reading the first section even if you skip the rest.

**One action is required: re-paste the companion app.** Details at the bottom.

---

## Your synced settings were readable by the wrong people

Hestia can sync your configuration through hestari.com so a new phone or tablet picks up your setup without you retyping it. That record was encrypted, but it was encrypted badly, and this is the honest description of how badly.

Your household's record is stored under a key derived from your home's public IP address. The encryption key was derived from **the same public IP plus a fixed piece of text written in Hestia's own source code**, which anyone can read, because the project is open source.

The consequence: anyone whose traffic leaves the internet from the same public IP address as yours could compute your encryption key and read your record. That record held your Maker API token, your Artemis PIN hash, your Settings PIN hash, your camera URLs, and your Anthropic and Google API keys.

If your home has its own public IP address, the practical risk was low, because sharing that address means being inside your house. **If your internet provider puts you behind CGNAT, which is increasingly common, you shared that address with a large number of unrelated homes**, and any of them could have done this. None of them had a reason to, and there is no evidence any of them did. It was still true, and it should not have been.

### What replaced it

Your household now has its own 256-bit secret, generated on your own device from your browser's cryptographic random number generator. It never goes to hestari.com. Encryption keys are derived from it with HKDF-SHA256 and the record is encrypted with AES-GCM-256.

The record is now split in two.

**The private half** holds everything sensitive, encrypted under your household's secret. **The servers have no way to decrypt it.** Not "are not supposed to" — there is no decryption path in the code, because a server that could still read everything would make the split cosmetic.

**The service half** is deliberately readable, and holds only what the servers genuinely need to answer one question: a door opened, should anyone be notified? That is your notification preferences, the IDs and names of the sensors and locks involved, and a hash of your Maker API token. It holds no tokens, no keys, no PINs and no camera URLs. The allow-list is explicit in the code, and there is a test suite whose entire job is to fail if anything else ever gets added to it.

### Writes are now authorised

Previously, anything that could reach the API from your IP address could overwrite your household's record. Now a write has to prove it holds your Maker API token. Your dashboard sends the token, the server hashes it, compares it against the hash already in your record, and discards it without storing it. A mismatch is rejected.

**This does mean your raw Maker API token is now sent to hestari.com on every settings save.** It is sent over HTTPS, compared, and thrown away. It is never written to storage, and the stored record holds only a hash. Saying so plainly is better than you finding out later: if that trade is not one you want, turn Cloud Sync off in Settings and Hestia works entirely against your hub.

If you rotate your Maker API token, your devices recover on their own. There is a separate recovery path so rotating a token cannot lock your household out of its own record.

## Your devices pair themselves

There is nothing to set up, no code to type and no QR code to scan.

Your household secret is distributed by **your hub**, through the companion app's config store, alongside the configuration your devices already read from it. A new device on your network gets the secret the same way it already gets everything else. The Wall Panel URL carries it too, so a kiosk tablet is covered.

A device that ends up with no secret does not silently fall back to the old, weak scheme and does not silently break. It keeps working against your hub and picks the secret up when it can reach it.

### Existing households migrate on their own

Your current record is read once under the old scheme, rewritten under the new one, and the old path is closed behind it. You do not have to do anything, and there is no window where your notifications stop. The code that reads old records is marked for deletion one release from now.

## A real screen when the connection drops

When Hestia lost contact with your hub, all you got was a red bar along the top, which is easy to miss on a wall-mounted tablet across the room.

After twenty seconds without a connection you now get an actual screen: what went wrong at the top, a refresh button in the middle, and the Hestia gem. It tells you which of the likely causes fits your setup rather than guessing, the same way the existing hub-unreachable banner does.

It deliberately stays out of the way during an alarm. If a countdown is running or the alarm is sounding, the security screen wins and the disconnect screen does not cover it.

## Also in this release

- **Diagnostics tells the truth about Cloud Sync and Push.** Both checks now perform a real round-trip instead of inferring from local state. The Push check re-registers this device as part of the check, which is safe to repeat, and reports a rejected registration as a failure rather than passing quietly.
- **The companion app preserves your household secret** when an older dashboard saves over your config. Without this, a browser tab left open on the previous version could erase the secret and split your household permanently.
- **The test suite can now fail.** It previously reported a missing test file as part of a green run and always exited successfully, which meant it could cover less than it claimed and nobody would know. A missing or crashed suite is now a hard failure. 290 tests across 9 suites.

## What you need to do

**Update the companion app first, and do it promptly.** This matters most if you run a wall panel or anything else served from your hub.

Your household secret lives in the companion app's config store. A device still running the old dashboard saves a config with no secret in it, and the old app stores that as-is, wiping the secret for every device. The new app detects this and carries the secret forward.

The reason it comes first is that your devices will not update together. Anything loading from hestari.com is on 2.0.0 the moment it reloads, while anything served from your hub stays on the old build until the companion app fetches the new one. That gap is precisely the mixed state described above. Updating the app closes it from both ends: it protects the secret, and it is what pulls the new dashboard down to your hub.

If every device in your household loads from hestari.com, this never really arises. Update the app regardless; it is one step.

**1. The companion app.** Either route works.

- **Via HPM:** Hubitat Package Manager, **Update**, tick **Hestia Dashboard**.
- **By hand:** copy all of `HestiaDashboard.groovy` from this release, then in Hubitat open **Apps Code** to **Hestia Dashboard**, select everything, paste over it, **Save**.

Pasting by hand is worth the ten seconds even for HPM users, because you see the version change rather than trusting that it applied.

**Then open the Hestia app itself and click ⬇ Update Dashboard File.** Easy to miss and it matters: saving the code in Apps Code does not pull the new dashboard down to your hub. That button calls downloadDashboard(true), a forced download. Done instead calls downloadDashboard(false), which checks build-info.json first, skips when it believes you are already current, and returns silently on any exception, so it can appear to work while leaving the hub on the old build. Clicking Done as well is harmless and is the only thing that re-subscribes to HSM events and reschedules the update check.

**2. The dashboard.** Nothing to do. On hestari.com it is already live, so reload. Served from your hub, the companion app downloads it for you as soon as you click Update Dashboard File above; skip that and it re-checks on its own within three hours.

**3. Confirm it.** Settings, Diagnostics, Run all checks.

Companion App is the check that matters. Green **"v2.0.0 (up to date)"** means the app and the dashboard agree, confirming both halves in one line. Amber showing two different versions means one side has not caught up.

Cloud Sync should read green "Synced". If it says **"Not migrated yet"**, that is expected immediately after updating and is not a fault: your stored record is still in the old format and gets rewritten on the next load, so reload once and it turns green.

**If you skip it:** nothing breaks right away, and your house keeps working regardless, because device control, arming, cameras and announcements all talk to your hub directly and never touch cloud sync. The exposure is limited to households running more than one device, where a device left on the old dashboard can wipe the household secret and leave devices disagreeing about which record they can read. Diagnostics reports it, and updating the app resolves it.

From now on, the companion app will be re-pasted on every version bump and its version number will always match the dashboard's. It has drifted behind in the past, which made it unclear whether an update had actually been applied.

Everything else is automatic. Your settings, your devices, your rooms, your PINs and your notification setup all carry over untouched.

## For the technically inclined

The threat model this addresses is the IP neighbour: someone who shares your public IP address, can reach the API, and has read the source code. Your own local network is deliberately inside the trust boundary, because the companion app already publishes your Maker API token unauthenticated at `/local/hestia-token.json` to anyone on your network. Fixing the sync record while leaving that in place would have been theatre, and the sync record is the part that reaches strangers.

Each derived value in the new design does exactly one job: the household secret encrypts, the token hash authorises writes, and a separate recovery verifier survives a token rotation. Eleven earlier designs were discarded during this work, and every one of them failed for the same reason, which was one value doing two jobs while an attacker could reach it.

The design was verified with a mutation sweep: 22 deliberate breaks were introduced into the security-critical lines one at a time, and the test suite caught all 22. A test suite that passes while the code is broken is not evidence of anything, and this was the cheapest way to find out whether these tests constrained the code or just agreed with it.
